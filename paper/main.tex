\subsection{Inverse problem}\label{sec:problem}

We consider the viscous Burgers equation on the periodic unit interval,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u + u\,\partial_x u = \nu\,\partial_x^2 u,\qquad x\in[0,1),\ t\in[0,T],
  \label{eq:burgers}
\end{equation}
with $u(x+1,t)=u(x,t)$ and initial condition $u(x,0)=\sin(2\pi x)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:burgers} is the simplest PDE that combines nonlinear advection with diffusion; the viscosity $\nu$ sets the competition between wave steepening and smoothing and therefore the stiffness of the problem~\cite{basdevant1986spectral}. The \emph{inverse problem} is to recover the unknown viscosity $\nu$ from sparse observations
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D=\{(x_i,t_i,u^\star_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},\qquad u^\star_i=u(x_i,t_i;\nu_{\rm true})+\varepsilon_i ,
  \label{eq:data}
\end{equation}
while simultaneously reconstructing the field $u(x,t)$. In the present work the observations are given by the exact Cole--Hopf solution (Appendix~\ref{app:colehopf}) with $\nu_{\rm true}=0.01$ and $\varepsilon_i=0$, for two final times: $T=0.1$, before the profile steepens, and $T=1$, after a shock-like front of width $\mathcal O(\nu)$ has formed at $x=1/2$ (the maximum of $u$ drops from 0.96 at $T=0.1$ to 0.33 at $T=1$). This problem is a controlled first step towards the seismic inverse problem, in which a wave speed $c(x)$ plays the role of $\nu$.

The residual of Eq.~\eqref{eq:burgers} is written as
\begin{equation}
  r(x,t;\nu)=A(x,t)-\nu\,B(x,t),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with $A=\partial_t u+u\,\partial_x u$ and $B=\partial_x^2 u$, which makes explicit that the unknown parameter enters \emph{linearly}. This property is exploited by the parameter update of Appendix~\ref{sec:varpro}.

\subsection{Physics-informed neural networks for inverse problems}\label{sec:pinn}

A physics-informed neural network (PINN)~\cite{raissi2019physics,karniadakis2021physics} approximates the solution by a parametrized function $u_\Theta(x,t)$ and penalizes the PDE residual together with the misfit to the data. In the inverse setting (PINNverse~\cite{PhysRevA.110.032607}) the physical parameter is promoted to an unknown of the optimization,
\begin{equation}
  (\Theta^\ast,\nu^\ast)=\arg\min_{\Theta,\nu}\; \mathcal L_{\rm res}[\Theta,\nu]+w_d\,\mathcal L_{\rm data}[\Theta].
  \label{eq:pinn_inverse}
\end{equation}
The data term fixes the particular solution and the residual term constrains $\nu$: for a network that reproduces the data, $\nu$ is identified by requiring $A-\nu B\simeq0$ throughout the domain.

Periodicity is imposed exactly by construction and, for $T=0.1$, so is the initial condition. The network acts on the periodic feature vector $\bm\xi(x,t)=(\sin2\pi x,\cos2\pi x,t/T)$ and the output is
\begin{equation}
  u_\Theta(x,t)=\sin(2\pi x)+\frac{t}{T}\,\big(\mathcal N_{\rm post}\circ\mathcal M\circ\mathcal N_{\rm pre}\big)\big(\bm\xi(x,t)\big),
  \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
so that no boundary or initial-condition loss is needed and Eq.~\eqref{eq:pinn_inverse} contains only two terms. For $T=1$ the hard constraint is replaced by a soft one (Sec.~\ref{sec:adjust}). In Eq.~\eqref{eq:composition} a classical pre-network $\mathcal N_{\rm pre}(\cdot;\bm\phi):\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^n$ maps the features to an $n$-dimensional latent vector $\bm\theta$, a middle block $\mathcal M$ transforms it, and a classical post-network $\mathcal N_{\rm post}(\cdot;\bm\psi):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ produces the correction to the initial profile. Both classical networks have one hidden layer of 10 $\tanh$ units and a linear output. The classical and hybrid PINNs differ \emph{only} in the middle block (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}a), so that any difference in performance can be attributed to it.

\subsection{Classical PINN}\label{sec:classical}

In the classical PINN the middle block is a trainable network,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M_{\rm C}(\bm\theta)=\mathcal N_{\rm mid}(\bm\theta;\bm\chi):\ \mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R^k,
  \label{eq:bottleneck}
\end{equation}
with one hidden layer of 10 $\tanh$ units, so that the full classical model is the $\tanh$ network $3\to10\to k\to10\to k\to10\to1$ with a bottleneck of width $k$. Matching the bottleneck to the number of qubits, $k=n$, makes the information flowing between the two classical networks pass through a latent space of the same dimension in both models. The classical model has $42k+71$ trainable parameters (197, 365 and 491 for $k=3,7,10$), of which $21k+10$ belong to the middle block (Table~\ref{tab:arch}).

\subsection{Hybrid quantum PINN with a fixed quantum layer}\label{sec:hqpinn}

In the hybrid model (HQPINN) the latent vector is used as the set of rotation angles of an $n$-qubit circuit,
\begin{equation}
  U(\bm\theta)=W_{\rm brick}^{D}\,\bigotimes_{q=1}^{n}R_Y(\theta_q),\qquad R_Y(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Y/2},
  \label{eq:circuit}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\rm brick}$ is one layer of a brickwork of nearest-neighbour CNOT gates (first on pairs $(1,2),(3,4),\dots$, then on $(2,3),(4,5),\dots$) and $D$ is the number of such layers; in this work $D=1$. The circuit acts on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and the layer output is the vector of local magnetizations,
\begin{equation}
  y_q(\bm\theta)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[Z_q\,\mathcal E_{\bm\theta}\big(|0\rangle\langle0|^{\otimes n}\big)\right],\qquad q=1,\dots,n,
  \label{eq:expval}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal E_{\bm\theta}$ is the (noisy) channel actually implemented by the device. On hardware $y_q$ is estimated from $S$ shots, $\hat y_q=y_q+\xi_q$, with $\mathbb E[\xi_q]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}[\xi_q]=(1-y_q^2)/S\le 1/S$.

The defining feature of the model is that \emph{the circuit has no free parameter}: all trainable parameters live in the classical networks, and the quantum layer acts as a fixed nonlinear map $\bm\theta\mapsto\bm y$. The HQPINN therefore has $21n+61$ trainable parameters (124, 208 and 271 for $n=3,7,10$), against $42k+71$ in the classical model of the same width. This is the opposite design choice to the variational hybrid PINNs studied so far~\cite{farea2025qcpinn,berger2025trainable,sedykh2024hybrid}, and connects the HQPINN to fixed-substrate approaches such as quantum reservoir computing and extreme learning machines~\cite{mujal2021opportunities} and quanvolutional networks~\cite{henderson2020quanvolutional}, which, however, have not been used to minimize a PDE residual.

\paragraph{Function class.} The structure of the quantum layer is captured by the following statement (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:trig}).
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:trig}
Let $\mathcal C_q\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ be the backward causal cone of $Z_q$ in the circuit~\eqref{eq:circuit}. For any noise model in which each gate is followed by a $\bm\theta$-independent, trace-preserving channel acting on the qubits of that gate (including readout errors), $y_q$ depends only on $\{\theta_j\}_{j\in\mathcal C_q}$ and is a multilinear trigonometric polynomial of degree one in each of them,
\begin{equation}
  y_q(\bm\theta)=\sum_{\bm\alpha\in\{0,c,s\}^{|\mathcal C_q|}}c^{(q)}_{\bm\alpha}\prod_{j\in\mathcal C_q}g_{\alpha_j}(\theta_j),
  \label{eq:trigpoly}
\end{equation}
with $g_0=1$, $g_c=\cos$, $g_s=\sin$, and device-dependent real coefficients $c^{(q)}_{\bm\alpha}$.
\end{proposition}
Equation~\eqref{eq:trigpoly} has three consequences that guide both the theory and the implementation. (i) The quantum layer injects a \emph{finite Fourier} structure into the latent representation~\cite{schuld2021effect}; the entangling depth $D$ controls which products of harmonics of different latent coordinates appear, so entanglement becomes a deliberate architectural knob rather than a by-product of training. (ii) Noise does not change the function class, only the coefficients; this is what allows a calibrated surrogate of the device (Appendix~\ref{sec:hil}). (iii) The quantum layer adds no trainable parameter, so the barren-plateau phenomenon of variational circuits~\cite{mcclean2018barren,cerezo2021cost,holmes2022connecting,ortizmarrero2021entanglement} is absent by construction; what remains is the possible concentration of the map $\bm\theta\mapsto\bm y$ itself, which is controlled by the size of the causal cones.
\begin{remark}\label{rem:parity}
A CNOT network acts on the computational basis as a linear map over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, represented by a binary matrix $M$. For the noiseless circuit~\eqref{eq:circuit} the Heisenberg evolution of $Z_q$ is therefore the Pauli string $Z_{S_q}=\prod_{j\in S_q}Z_j$ with $S_q=\{j: M_{qj}=1\}\subseteq\mathcal C_q$, and
\begin{equation}
  y_q(\bm\theta)=\prod_{j\in S_q}\cos\theta_j .
  \label{eq:parity}
\end{equation}
For angles spread over a full period, $\operatorname{Var}[y_q]=2^{-|S_q|}$ and the gradients $\partial y_q/\partial\theta_j$ are suppressed by the same factor: the entangling depth trades expressivity (longer products) against concentration. Equation~\eqref{eq:parity} also defines the natural \emph{classical control} for the HQPINN, namely a middle block with cosine-product activations, which must be included in the comparison \TODO{rodar este controle}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Training algorithm}\label{sec:training}

Both models are trained with the same algorithm, sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}(b) and given in full in Appendix~\ref{app:training} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:train}); only the hyperparameters, selected independently for each model with the same protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), differ. At every epoch a fresh batch of $N$ collocation points is drawn, and the same points enter both the PDE residual, evaluated by second-order finite differences on a five-point stencil, and the data term. The networks are updated with AdamW, with 16 steps per epoch on the batch (for the HQPINN, all with the same device measurement), on the mean absolute error of the residual, whose weight is ramped up from zero, plus a fixed-weight data term [Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}]. The viscosity is not updated by gradient descent: since it enters the residual linearly, its $L_1$-optimal value for the current network---the $|B_i|$-weighted median of $A_i/B_i$---is obtained in closed form [Eq.~\eqref{eq:wmed}], and $\log\nu$ is relaxed towards it (variable projection). For the HQPINN the device is measured once per epoch, at the stencil centres only; the neighbouring stencil nodes and the gradients are obtained from a calibrated surrogate of the device based on Eq.~\eqref{eq:trigpoly} [Eq.~\eqref{eq:ste}], which is refitted periodically with the accumulated measurements. This construction avoids both parameter-shift evaluations and the $h^{-2}$ amplification of the shot noise by the finite differences.

\subsection{Training at the larger final time}\label{sec:adjust}
At $T=1$ the algorithm above fails to represent the solution: with the hard constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eq:composition} the network has to cancel $\sin(2\pi x)$ linearly in $t$ while forming the front, and after 110 epochs the classical model reconstructs $u(x,T)$ with a relative $L_2$ error of 0.2--0.4. For this case only, and identically for both models, three changes are made: (i) the network output is $u$ itself, and the initial condition enters as a third loss term, the mean absolute error at $N$ random points of $t=0$ per epoch with weight $w_0=10$ (for the HQPINN these points are measured in the same job as the collocation centres, so the number of jobs is unchanged); (ii) during the first $K_0=10$ epochs only the data term is used and $\nu$ is not updated, after which the residual weight is ramped up; (iii) the learning rate of the networks is kept constant and divided by 1.5 whenever the epoch loss has not decreased by 1\% for 5 epochs, instead of following a cosine decay. These changes were selected on the classical model among 147 variants of the training (other optimizers, including L-BFGS; loss-balancing schemes~\cite{wang2021understanding}; learning-rate rules driven by the loss; warm-up lengths), on 60 runs with seeds not used in this paper: the median $\epsilon_\nu$ dropped from 38.5\% to 12.7\% and the median $L_2$ error of $u(x,T)$ from 0.23 to 0.067. On a proxy of the device (calibrated surrogate plus shot noise) the same changes reduce the median $\epsilon_\nu$ of the HQPINN from 32.7\% to 20.7\%; no other change of its hyperparameters gave a significant further gain, whereas more collocation points per epoch did (10.5\% with $N=20$, 4.2\% with $N=40$). Here $N=10$ is kept for both models.

\section{Exact solution of the benchmark}\label{app:colehopf}
The Cole--Hopf transformation~\cite{cole1951quasi,hopf1950partial} $u=-2\nu\,\partial_x\ln\varphi$ maps Eq.~\eqref{eq:burgers} onto the heat equation $\partial_t\varphi=\nu\,\partial_x^2\varphi$. For $u(x,0)=\sin(2\pi x)$,
\begin{equation}
  \varphi(x,0)=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\nu}\int_0^x\sin(2\pi s)\,ds\right]\propto e^{z\cos(2\pi x)},
\end{equation}
with $z=1/(4\pi\nu)$. Using $e^{z\cos\vartheta}=I_0(z)+2\sum_{m\ge1}I_m(z)\cos m\vartheta$, with $I_m$ the modified Bessel functions, each Fourier mode decays as $e^{-4\pi^2m^2\nu t}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u(x,t)=\frac{8\pi\nu\sum_{m\ge1}m\,I_m(z)\,e^{-4\pi^2m^2\nu t}\sin(2\pi m x)}{I_0(z)+2\sum_{m\ge1}I_m(z)\,e^{-4\pi^2m^2\nu t}\cos(2\pi m x)}.
  \label{eq:colehopf}
\end{equation}
The series is evaluated in double precision with exponentially scaled Bessel functions $I_m(z)e^{-z}$ (the common factor cancels) and $m<4z+64$ terms. It reproduces the initial condition to machine precision and satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:burgers} to the accuracy of a finite-difference check ($\sim10^{-5}$); for $\nu=0.01$, $\max_x u\simeq0.961$ at $T=0.1$ and $0.331$ at $T=1$. The series loses precision for $z\gtrsim16$ ($\nu\lesssim5\times10^{-3}$), where the Gauss--Hermite integral representation~\cite{basdevant1986spectral} must be used instead---a constraint for the stiffness sweep of Fig.~\ref{fig:regime}.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:trig} and light-cone structure}\label{app:trig}
\emph{Trigonometric structure.} For a single rotation,
\begin{align}
  R_Y(\theta)\,\rho\,R_Y^\dagger(\theta)={}&\tfrac12(\rho+Y\rho Y)+\tfrac12\cos\theta\,(\rho-Y\rho Y)\nonumber\\
  &-\tfrac{i}{2}\sin\theta\,[Y,\rho],
  \label{eq:ry_channel}
\end{align}
so the map $\rho\mapsto R_Y(\theta)\rho R_Y^\dagger(\theta)$ is an affine combination of $\{1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta\}$ with $\theta$-independent superoperators. All other operations---CNOTs, noise channels, readout confusion matrices, and the trace with $Z_q$---are linear and $\theta$-independent. Since each angle appears in exactly one gate, $y_q$ is a sum of products in which every $\theta_j$ contributes exactly one factor in $\{1,\cos\theta_j,\sin\theta_j\}$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:trigpoly}.

\emph{Causal cone.} In the Heisenberg picture $y_q=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_0\,\mathcal E^\dagger_{\bm\theta}(Z_q)]$. The adjoint of a trace-preserving channel is unital, so every gate (and every gate-local noise channel) whose support does not intersect the current support of the evolved operator leaves it unchanged. The support therefore grows only through gates touching it; the set of qubits reached is $\mathcal C_q$, and rotations outside $\mathcal C_q$ drop out. For the brickwork with $D$ layers, $|\mathcal C_q|\le\min(n,4D)$ \TODO{verificar contagem exata para a ordem das subcamadas usada}. Crosstalk or non-local noise invalidates this bound and enlarges the effective cone.

\emph{Noiseless case (Remark~\ref{rem:parity}).} CNOT conjugation maps $Z_c\mapsto Z_c$ and $Z_t\mapsto Z_cZ_t$, so $Z_q$ evolves into a $Z$-string $Z_{S_q}$ whose support is the $q$th row of the GF(2) matrix $M$ of the network. Acting on $\bigotimes_jR_Y(\theta_j)|0\rangle$, $\langle Z_j\rangle=\cos\theta_j$ and the state is a product, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:parity}. If $\theta_j$ are independent and uniform on a period, $\mathbb E[\cos^2\theta_j]=1/2$, hence $\operatorname{Var}[y_q]=2^{-|S_q|}$ and $\mathbb E[(\partial_{\theta_j}y_q)^2]=2^{-|S_q|}$.

\emph{Number of coefficients.} Equation~\eqref{eq:trigpoly} has $3^{|\mathcal C_q|}$ coefficients per output, which fixes the minimum number of calibration samples and motivates shallow entangling layers.

\section{Training algorithm}\label{app:training}
Both models are trained with Algorithm~\ref{alg:train}; for the classical model the branches marked HQPINN are skipped. The four ingredients---collocation and finite differences, the network step, the variable-projection step for $\nu$, and the quantum processor in the loop---are described below, followed by the implementation details of the hardware loop.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Inverse (H)QPINN training}\label{alg:train}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require epochs $K$, batch size $N$, stencil $(h,\tau)$, schedules $\eta_k,\beta_k,\rho_k$, weight $w_d$, shots $S$, refit period $K_{\rm fit}$
\State \textbf{HQPINN:} calibration job $\to$ fit surrogate $\hat f$ [Eq.~\eqref{eq:trigpoly}]
\State initialize $\Theta$; draw $\nu_0\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$
\For{$k=0,\dots,K-1$}
  \State draw centres $\{(x_i,t_i)\}_{i=1}^N$; build stencil nodes $\mathcal S$
  \State $\bm\theta\gets\mathcal N_{\rm pre}(\bm\xi(\mathcal S))$
  \If{HQPINN}
     \If{$k>0$ \textbf{and} $k\bmod K_{\rm fit}=0$} refit $\hat f$ \EndIf
     \State $\hat{\bm y}_c\gets$ QPU$(\bm\theta_c;S)$ \Comment{one job, centres only}
     \State $\tilde{\bm y}\gets\hat f(\bm\theta)+\sg[\hat{\bm y}_c-\hat f(\bm\theta_c)]$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ste}}
  \Else \State $\tilde{\bm y}\gets\mathcal N_{\rm mid}(\bm\theta)$
  \EndIf
  \State $u\gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:composition}; \ $A_i,B_i\gets$ FD$(u)$
  \State $\Theta\gets$ AdamW$\big(\nabla_\Theta\mathcal L_k;\eta_k\big)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}}
  \State $\nu^\star\gets\wmed(\{A_i/B_i\};\{|B_i|\})$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:wmed}}
  \State $\log\nu_{k+1}\gets\log\nu_k+\beta_k(\log\nu^\star-\log\nu_k)$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\hat\nu=\nu_K$, $u_\Theta$; validation job on $u(x,T)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Collocation and finite-difference residual}\label{sec:fd}
At every epoch $k$ a fresh batch of $N$ points is drawn uniformly, $x_i\in[0,1)$ and $t_i\in[\tau,T-\tau]$. The \emph{same} points are used for the residual and for the data term, so that each evaluation of the model enters both. The derivatives in Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual} are computed by second-order central differences on the five-point cross stencil $\{(x_i,t_i),(x_i\pm h,t_i),(x_i,t_i\pm\tau)\}$ with $h=1/512$ and $\tau=10^{-3}$ (see below); stencil nodes are wrapped modulo 1 in $x$. Finite differences, rather than automatic differentiation, are used because hardware outputs are not differentiable with respect to the inputs, and because all stencil nodes of a batch can be evaluated in a single pass.

\emph{Stencil formulas.} On the cross stencil,
\begin{align}
  \partial_tu&\approx\frac{u(x,t+\tau)-u(x,t-\tau)}{2\tau},\qquad \partial_xu\approx\frac{u_{+}-u_{-}}{2h},\nonumber\\
  \partial_x^2u&\approx\frac{u_{+}-2u_0+u_{-}}{h^2},\qquad u_\pm=u(x\pm h,t),
\end{align}
with truncation errors $\tfrac{\tau^2}{6}\partial_t^3u$, $\tfrac{h^2}{6}\partial_x^3u$ and $\tfrac{h^2}{12}\partial_x^4u$; for $h=1/512$ and $\tau=10^{-3}$ these are negligible compared with the other errors of the method.

\emph{Why the stencil cannot be measured.} If each node carried independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma_u$ (shot noise $\le1/\sqrt S$ in $y_q$ propagated through $\mathcal N_{\rm post}$), the noise of the estimates would be
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{std}[\partial_xu]=\frac{\sigma_u}{\sqrt2\,h},\qquad \operatorname{std}[\partial_x^2u]=\frac{\sqrt6\,\sigma_u}{h^2}.
\end{equation}
With $h=1/512$ and $\sigma_u\sim10^{-2}$ this gives $\operatorname{std}[\partial_x^2u]\sim6\times10^3$, four orders of magnitude above $|\partial_x^2u|\lesssim(2\pi)^2$. Enlarging $h$ to make this tolerable would introduce a truncation bias in $B$ and hence in $\nu^\star$.

\emph{Shared offset.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:ste} assigns the same correction $\Delta_c=\hat{\bm y}^{\,\rm QPU}(\bm\theta_c)-\hat f(\bm\theta_c)$ to the five nodes of the stencil. Writing $\tilde{\bm y}_\pm=\hat f(\bm\theta_\pm)+\Delta_c$ and expanding $\mathcal N_{\rm post}$,
\begin{equation}
  u_+-u_-=J(\hat f_c+\Delta_c)\,\big[\hat f(\bm\theta_+)-\hat f(\bm\theta_-)\big]+\mathcal O(h^3),
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the Jacobian of $\mathcal N_{\rm post}$ (times $t/T$). The noise enters only through the evaluation point of $J$, so it perturbs the derivatives by a relative amount $\mathcal O(\|J'\|\,\|\Delta_c\|)$ instead of $\mathcal O(\sigma_u/h^2)$; the variation across the stencil is that of the smooth surrogate. The measured value enters directly the data term and the factor $u$ of $u\,\partial_xu$.

\subsection{Loss and network step}
The network parameters $\Theta=(\bm\phi,\bm\psi)$ [plus $\bm\chi$ in the classical model] are updated with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} on
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_k(\Theta)=\rho_k\,\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\big|A_i-\nu_kB_i\big|+w_d\,\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\big|u_\Theta(x_i,t_i)-u^\star_i\big|,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_k$ is held fixed. The mean absolute error is preferred to the mean squared error because it is robust to the heavy-tailed residuals produced by a few badly resolved collocation points and by measurement noise. The residual weight $\rho_k=\min\{1,(k+1)/K_{\rm ramp}\}$ is ramped up so that the networks first fit the data, and the data weight $w_d\gg1$ is fixed. The learning rate follows a cosine decay~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} and the gradient norm is clipped.

\subsection{Variable projection for the viscosity}\label{sec:varpro}
Because $\nu$ enters the residual linearly [Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual}], for a fixed network it can be eliminated in closed form (variable projection~\cite{golub1973differentiation}). Under the $L_1$ norm of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss},
\begin{equation}
  \nu^\star_k=\arg\min_{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^N|A_i-\nu B_i|=\wmed\!\left(\left\{\frac{A_i}{B_i}\right\};\ \{|B_i|\}\right),
  \label{eq:wmed}
\end{equation}
the median of the ratios $A_i/B_i$ weighted by $|B_i|$ (proof below), computed from the same forward pass as the network step and clipped to $[10^{-4},10]$. Rather than jumping to this batch estimate, which fluctuates from batch to batch, the viscosity is relaxed towards it in logarithmic scale,
\begin{equation}
  \log\nu_{k+1}=\log\nu_k+\beta_k\big(\log\nu^\star_k-\log\nu_k\big),
  \label{eq:logrelax}
\end{equation}
i.e., $\nu_{k+1}=\nu_k^{1-\beta_k}(\nu^\star_k)^{\beta_k}$, with $\beta_k$ decaying from $\beta_0$ to $\beta_K$ with a cosine schedule. Equation~\eqref{eq:logrelax} is an exponentially weighted geometric average of the batch estimates; it keeps $\nu>0$, is scale invariant, and its effective memory $\sim1/\beta_k$ grows during training, damping the noise of late batches. The initial value $\nu_0$ is drawn uniformly in $(0,1)$.

\emph{Weighted-median solution.}
Write $\sum_i|A_i-\nu B_i|=\sum_iw_i|r_i-\nu|$ with $r_i=A_i/B_i$ and $w_i=|B_i|$ (points with $B_i=0$ do not depend on $\nu$ and are discarded). This is a convex piecewise-linear function of $\nu$ whose subgradient is $\sum_{r_i<\nu}w_i-\sum_{r_i>\nu}w_i+[-1,1]\sum_{r_i=\nu}w_i$. It contains zero when the weight on each side of $\nu$ is at most half of the total, i.e., at the weighted median of $\{r_i\}$. Points with small curvature $|B_i|$, for which the ratio $A_i/B_i$ is ill-conditioned, receive proportionally small weight. Under the $L_2$ norm the minimizer would instead be $\nu^\star=\sum_iA_iB_i/\sum_iB_i^2$, which is not robust to outliers. In the implementation the ratios are sorted, the cumulative weight is formed, and the first ratio at which it reaches half of the total is returned; the result is clipped to $[10^{-4},10]$ before Eq.~\eqref{eq:logrelax}.

\subsection{Hardware in the loop}\label{sec:hil}
Running the HQPINN on a quantum processor requires a gradient of the measured outputs with respect to $\bm\theta$, and the finite-difference stencil of Sec.~\ref{sec:fd} would multiply the number of circuits by five. Both problems are solved by Proposition~\ref{prop:trig}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Calibration.} Before training, one job per circuit size evaluates the circuit at random angles $\theta_j\sim\mathcal U(-\pi,\pi)$ on every $n$-qubit chain of the chip in parallel. The measured $\langle Z_q\rangle$ are fitted by the trigonometric polynomial~\eqref{eq:trigpoly}, which yields a surrogate $\hat f_q(\bm\theta_{\mathcal C_q})$ of the device; chains whose error score or calibration misfit is anomalous are discarded (Sec.~\ref{sec:hil_impl}).
  \item \emph{Training.} At each epoch the device is measured only at the $N$ stencil centres $\bm\theta_c$. Every node $\bm\theta$ of the stencil centred at $c$ is then assigned
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{\bm y}(\bm\theta)=\hat f(\bm\theta)+\sg\!\big[\hat{\bm y}^{\,\rm QPU}(\bm\theta_c)-\hat f(\bm\theta_c)\big],
    \label{eq:ste}
  \end{equation}
  where $\sg$ stops the gradient (straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013estimating}). At the centre $\tilde{\bm y}=\hat{\bm y}^{\,\rm QPU}$, so the data term and the factor $u$ in $u\,\partial_xu$ use the measured value; across the stencil the variation of $\tilde{\bm y}$ is that of the smooth surrogate, so that the shot noise is \emph{not} amplified by the $h^{-2}$ of the finite differences (see below); and $\partial\tilde{\bm y}/\partial\bm\theta=\partial\hat f/\partial\bm\theta$. Every $K_{\rm fit}$ epochs the surrogate is refitted with the calibration data and all measurements accumulated so far.
  \item \emph{Validation.} A final job evaluates the trained model on 100 equally spaced points at $t=T$, with the circuit run on the device.
\end{enumerate}
With the calibration shared by all runs of a given size, a training run of $K$ epochs costs $K+1$ jobs. Implementation details follow.

\subsection{Implementation of the hardware loop}\label{sec:hil_impl}
\paragraph{Chains and calibration.} All disjoint chains of $n$ connected qubits are identified on the coupling map, and the circuit is laid out on all of them in parallel, so that a single job yields one sample per chain. Chains whose error score (from the device calibration data) exceeds $0.05\,n$ are discarded. The calibration job evaluates $\approx1024$ random angle configurations per output, $\theta_j\sim\mathcal U(-\pi,\pi)$; the coefficients $c^{(q)}_{\bm\alpha}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:trigpoly} are obtained by linear least squares on the features $\bigotimes_{j\in\mathcal C_q}(1,\cos\theta_j,\sin\theta_j)$, fitted jointly over the chains, and chains whose misfit exceeds three times the median are discarded. For \texttt{FakeBerlin} this retains 33, 14 and 9 chains for $n=3,7,10$, with misfit at the shot-noise floor. The calibration is cached and shared by all runs of a given size. \TODO{detalhar \texttt{find\_chains}, \texttt{screen\_chains} e \texttt{fit\_surrogate} (Inverser\_quantum\_hardware\_pinn.py).}

\paragraph{Training.} One job per epoch evaluates the $N=10$ stencil centres (at $T=1$, also the 10 points of the initial-condition loss) with $S=1024$ shots each (precision $1/32$ per expectation value). Gradients follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:ste}. Every $K_{\rm fit}=10$ epochs the surrogate is refitted with the calibration data and all measurements accumulated during training.

\paragraph{Validation.} One job evaluates $u(x,T)$ on 100 points, with one replica of each point per chain; the replicas are averaged. Three versions are reported: the model with the circuit run on the device ($\epsilon_{L_2}^{\rm QPU}$), the model with the surrogate it was trained through, and the same weights with the ideal circuit.

\paragraph{Device model, real hardware and cost.} \texttt{FakeBerlin} reproduces the calibrated gate and readout errors and the coupling map of \texttt{ibm\_berlin}, but not crosstalk, temporal drift, or the error mitigation of the runtime service. On real hardware the circuits are packed into a runtime \texttt{Session} (limit 2\,h per run) with optional error mitigation \TODO{qual}. A run uses 111 jobs (110 training + 1 validation) besides the shared calibration. Wall-clock time per run on the simulator, with 12 runs sharing 16 CPU cores, is 263--538\,s ($n=3,7$) and 666--1450\,s ($n=10$), versus 2--12\,s for the classical model.
